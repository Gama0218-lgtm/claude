# DCAS/BISG Methodology Brief
## *SGT George Ramos: The Mathematics of Vietnam*
### A Note on Classification, Chronology, and the 349 Figure

**Prepared by:** Gabriel Arce, PhD Candidate, USC Sol Price School of Public Policy  
**Date:** May 29, 2026

---

### The Problem Is Not Misclassification. It Is Pre-Classification.

The Vietnam Conflict Extract Data File of the Defense Casualty Analysis System (DCAS) records 58,220 U.S. military fatal casualties. Of these, 349 are coded Hispanic — approximately 0.6% of total casualties.

This figure is not the product of deliberate misclassification. It is the product of a data system built before the federal government had a standard definition of “Hispanic.”

On **May 12, 1977**, the Office of Management and Budget issued Statistical Policy Directive No. 15 — the first time in U.S. history that “Hispanic” was established as a uniform federal ethnic category, required for use across all federal agencies in the collection and presentation of data. Before that date, no consistent federal standard existed for identifying or counting persons of Latin American or Spanish origin in administrative records.

The Vietnam War ran from 1964 to 1975. The casualties it generated were recorded before the category existed.

The 349 figure does not represent a count of Hispanic casualties. It represents the upper bound of what could be identified under pre-1977 classification practices — a system that had no uniform definition, no standardized collection requirement, and no methodology for retrospective ethnic assignment.

---

### What the Audit Measures

The audit conducted for this manuscript applies the Bayesian Improved Surname Geocoding (BISG) methodology — developed by the RAND Corporation (RR-1162) and adopted as the standard methodology for ethnic population estimation by the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau in fair-lending enforcement — retroactively to the DCAS casualty universe.

BISG does not require self-reported ethnicity. It estimates ethnic probability from surname distributions and, where available, geographic data. Applied to the 58,220 DCAS records, it surfaces an estimated 2,309 to 3,000 casualties with high probability of Hispanic origin — a gap of 84.9% between what was coded and what the government’s own current methodology would identify.

This is not a claim that the original recorders lied. It is a measurement of what happens when you apply 2014 federal standards to 1970 federal data: the invisibility becomes legible.

---

### Why This Matters Methodologically

The argument is not “the government misclassified Hispanic veterans.” The argument is more precise and more difficult to dismiss:

> *The government’s own casualty records were created before the government had a standard definition of Hispanic. When measured by the government’s own current federal standards — the same standards used to enforce fair-lending law — the demographic gap becomes quantifiable. The failure is not in the original recording. The failure is that in fifty years, no one corrected the record.*

The 11–12 Puerto Rican surnames identified within the 349-coded records — present in the data but without Hispanic ethnic designation — confirm that the classification gap is not uniform zero: some Hispanic casualties were captured, inconsistently, by whatever ad hoc practices existed before Directive 15. The remainder were not captured at all.
